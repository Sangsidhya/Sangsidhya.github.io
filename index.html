Causal Structure Meets Stackelberg Games: Exploiting Known Causal Graphs for Efficient Equilibrium Learning
2025learning theorycausal inferencegame theoryUnder review
S. Kar, B. Chang, J. Loi, W. Chang, A. Parafita
Abstract

We study a two-player Stackelberg game in which both players intervene on variables in a shared causal DAG. Each player's reward is generated by an unknown node of their subgraph, and identifying this node reduces their action space from \(\Theta(nK)\) interventions to \(K\). While the single-agent problem admits an \(O(\log n)\) binary search using a fixed do-nothing baseline, this baseline becomes endogenous in the game because it depends on the opponent's actions.

We propose a concurrent identification protocol that preserves the validity of these tests without coordination. Both players explore uniformly and bucket observations by joint action, ensuring that ancestor tests are performed with the opponent's action fixed. When one player identifies its reward node, it continues uniform exploration over the reduced action set rather than committing to a best response, shrinking the effective opponent action space for the remaining search. We derive a Stackelberg regret bound of \(t_0+\tilde{O}(\sqrt{KT})\), where \(t_0\) is independent of the horizon \(T\), so the leading term depends on \(K\) rather than the causal graph size.

Simulations closely match the predicted identification rates and transition savings. However, the identification cost scales as \(n_l n_f K^2\gamma_{\mathrm{id}}^{-2}\), making the protocol impractical on the instances studied; reducing this dependence is the main challenge for practical use.

A Functional Scaling Law for One-Pass SGD with Feature Learning
2026learning theoryscaling lawsUnder review
S. Kar, M. Sangal, W. Chang, D. Kim, B. Li
Abstract

The Functional Scaling Law (FSL) of Li et al. (2026) characterises the expected-loss trajectory of one-pass SGD under arbitrary learning-rate schedules when features are frozen at initialisation. In contrast, Bordelon et al. (2025) show that feature learning improves the loss exponent from \(t^{-s}\) to \(t^{-2s/(1+s)}\) for hard tasks (\(s<1\)), but provide only leading-order asymptotics for constant learning rates and noiseless targets. We extend the FSL to feature learning by showing that, under a dynamical mean-field theory (DMFT) closure assumption, the loss retains the lazy FSL's functional form under the effective-time transformation

\[ \tilde t \asymp t+\gamma^{1/(1+s)}\,t^{2-\chi}, \qquad \chi = s\max\{1,\,2/(1+s)\}, \]

with the noise-rate schedule transformed to \(\tilde\rho=\rho\,dt/d\tilde t\). Here \(\gamma>0\) is the feature-learning rate, and \(\chi=2s/(1+s)\) for \(s<1\). For gradient flow from a balanced initialisation, we derive the clock from an exact conservation law of the two-layer dynamics. The resulting square-root dependence of the clock rate on the norm of the learned function determines the \(\gamma^{1/(1+s)}\) scaling and yields the loss prefactor \(\gamma^{-s/(1+s)}\), in contrast to \(\gamma^{-s}\) in Bordelon et al. (2025). The law recovers the lazy FSL as \(\gamma\to 0\) and the feature-learning exponent in the gradient-flow limit, while also characterising schedule-dependent SGD transients. At constant learning rate, feature learning amplifies the effective noise rate, causing the SGD noise contribution to grow rather than saturate. Simulations also show that large label noise slows the effective clock. We derive the expected clock drift under noise and propose a heuristic closure that captures its turnover and the continued loss reduction beyond the plateau predicted by the noise-free clock.

Our proof follows the seven-step argument of Li et al. (2026). All steps extend unconditionally except the reduction of the residual SDE to a scalar Volterra equation, which requires the stated DMFT closure. In gradient flow from an aligned balanced initialisation, we establish the law without this assumption by bounding the effect of the rank-one correction from the conservation law. Simulations validate the predictions in the solvable model and show effective-time collapse in a tanh MLP and a small transformer language model. In the solvable model, the schedule optimised using the feature-learning law improves on cosine by 15% at \(\gamma=1\), whereas the schedule optimised using the lazy law incurs a loss 37% higher than that choice.

When Known Objects Hide Unknowns: Sparse-Contamination Theory for CLIP-Based OOD Detection
2026statisticsmachine learningOOD detectionUnder review
D. Liu, S. Kar, R. Piao, M. Burenko, W. Chang
Abstract

Existing CLIP-based out-of-distribution (OOD) detectors generally formulate novelty as a global image-level distributional shift: an image is either entirely ID or entirely OOD. This formulation does not explicitly distinguish images in which an unknown object co-occurs with recognizable in-distribution (ID) content. Under a normalized linear pooling model, we derive a lower bound on the global MCM score and give sufficient conditions under which an image containing unknown content is nevertheless accepted as ID (masking theorem). These conditions depend on the unknown region's pooling mass, the patch margins, the pooled feature norm, and the detector's acceptance threshold. We formulate mixed-image OOD detection as structured sparse hypothesis testing over local CLIP patch features and derive finite-sample sufficient detection conditions for global sum, patch maximum, penalized adaptive top-\(k\), and spatial scan tests under independent sub-Gaussian noise. Exact Gaussian power formulas clarify the dependence of the sum and maximum on contamination size.

For independent Gaussian noise, we prove a lower bound using disjoint candidate regions that matches the scan upper bound in rate when the logarithms of the packing size and search-family size are comparable, and a truncated second-moment argument shows that the detection constant \(\delta\sqrt{k}=\sqrt{2\log J}\) is sharp for the fixed-size scan when the two logarithms are asymptotically equal. We also give a distribution-free finite-sample guarantee for a clipped scan that requires no moment conditions on the background noise. These results motivate our development of an adaptive post-hoc detector, SpatialPMIScan, that requires no retraining, no bounding-box annotations, and no additional models beyond a frozen CLIP encoder, while providing finite-sample false-positive control through split conformal calibration.

Through Monte Carlo experiments, we examine the predicted dependence of detection power on object size and signal strength and identify background tails, support geometry, and evidence dispersion as additional determinants of aggregation performance. We show that clipping patch scores substantially improves scan power under skewed and heavy-tailed backgrounds while retaining strong Gaussian performance. In image-level evaluations, we measure weak local evidence for small pasted unknown regions and find that the global MCM and GL-MCM scores lead spatial scanning across the VOC area strata.

Honest Finite-sample Inference for Wasserstein Distances in General Dimension
2026SSRNstatisticsoptimal transport
Sangsidhya Kar
Abstract

We study confidence intervals and tests for the Wasserstein distance \(W_p(P,Q)\) between two distributions on \(\mathbb{R}^d\), \(d\ge 2\), observed through independent samples, under a non-asymptotic, uniform-coverage (honest) criterion. Three groups of results are obtained.

First, certified inference. Kantorovich duality on a held-out sample gives a lower confidence bound for \(W_1\) with finite-sample coverage for every pair of Borel probability measures, in every dimension, without moment, support or smoothness assumptions. A single pointwise inequality, that the empirical cost dominates the empirical mean of the population dual potential, gives upper confidence bounds under a known support bound or under a \(q\)-th moment bound alone.

Second, sharp limits for the distance as a functional of two unknown distributions. Without any a priori control every honest upper bound is infinite with probability at least \(1-\alpha\). Under moment conditions alone the sharp one-sided rate is \(M^{1/q}\{n^{-1/d}\vee n^{-(q-1)/q}\}\). Over all measures on a bounded set, and over Sobolev classes of order \(s\), each side of any honest interval must pay \((n\log n)^{-1/d}\) and \(n^{-(s+1)/(2s+d)}\) respectively, up to logarithms, which the primal-dual and smoothed primal-dual intervals attain. At \(P=Q\) the sharp excess of honest upper bounds is \(n^{-2/d}\vee n^{-1/2}\) over bounded densities and \(n^{-2(1+s)/(4s+d)}\vee n^{-1/2}\) over Sobolev ellipsoids, the latter being a local rate at every pair whose transport plan leaves an open region untouched.

Third, adaptation. The lower side admits parametric excess at any fixed pair, so its uniform \(n^{-1/d}\) excess is a price of not knowing the dual potential, and this price vanishes under semi-discrete structure or smoothness of the potential.

Why Adaptive Optimizers Underestimate Rare Tokens
2026arXivlearning theoryoptimization
Sangsidhya Kar
Abstract

In the softmax output layer, a rare token receives a small positive logit gradient on most steps and a much larger negative gradient on the few steps when it is the target. SGD simply adds these contributions. Coordinate-wise adaptive methods such as Adam, RMSProp, and sign descent instead divide each update by a running estimate of its magnitude, and that estimate is largest immediately after the token appears. This imbalance has two effects.

First, preservation of the mean output embedding. At the level of the whole output layer, we characterize which optimizers preserve the mean output embedding: every method whose update is linear in past gradients does, as do Kronecker-factored and orthogonalized methods such as Shampoo and Muon. Adam, Adafactor, Lion, and sign descent do not, and for these methods we obtain an exact step-by-step expression for the change.

Second, bias in rare-token probabilities. At the level of an individual rare token, the same normalization shifts the training fixed point. In the unigram model, sign descent lowers the logit of every token that occurs in fewer than half of the minibatches at a constant expected rate. For RMSProp with periodic arrivals, we can solve the fixed point in closed form: if a token is absent for at least two consecutive minibatches, its equilibrium probability is strictly below its data frequency for every learning rate, and the ratio tends to \(\kappa/(2(e^{\kappa/2}-1))\). Here \(\kappa\) is the mean number of steps between occurrences divided by the second-moment time constant \(1/(1-\beta_2)\). In the same model, SGD and AMSGrad retain the unbiased fixed point.

Third, empirical validation. We test these predictions both in a unigram model and in a small language model trained from a known generating distribution. With random arrivals, the bias is larger than the periodic formula predicts; in the language model, the optimizers with the biased fixed point also fit the generating distribution less well.

A Dynamic Central Limit Theorem for Mean-Field Quantum Filtering
2026arXivprobabilityquantum filtering
Sangsidhya Kar, Vassili N. Kolokoltsov
Abstract

We study Open Problem 3 of Kolokoltsov's survey Quantum filtering and propagation of chaos for open quantum systems (2026) on \(\mathscr{H}=\mathbb{C}^{d}\) (\(2\le d<\infty\) fixed): a dynamic central limit theorem for \(N\) mean-field coupled quantum particles under continuous diffusive (homodyne) measurement. The tagged conditional state \(\Gamma^{(1)}_{t}\) is compared with the one-particle filter \(\gamma_{t}\) driven by its own innovation, the mean-field Hamiltonian evaluated at the deterministic Hartree-Lindblad mean \(\eta_{t}\).

The fluctuation \(F^{N}_{t}=\sqrt{N}\,(\Gamma^{(1)}_{t}-\gamma_{t})\) is not autonomous in the limit: it is driven through the linearised filter by its own innovation, by an idiosyncratic Gaussian martingale carried by the connected correlations with the other particles, and by the empirical field \(\widehat{G}^{N}_{t}=\sqrt{N}\,(\overline{\Gamma}_{t}-\eta_{t})\), which itself satisfies an autonomous linear Gaussian equation, so that \((F,\widehat{G})\) solves a closed system. Unconditionally, \((F^{N},\widehat{G}^{N})\) is tight, every limit point solves this system driven by a Brownian motion orthogonal to a square-integrable martingale pair, and the mean covariance of the idiosyncratic martingale is identified as the limit of the rescaled pair correlations.

The remaining clauses, concerning signs rather than amplitudes, reduce to a single decorrelation statement fixing the law of the driving pair. It survives in a conditional form, with the common response to the empirical field and the tagged-slot component projected out; without those projections it is false for generic data, which is what fixes them. The conditional form is proved at leading order on a shrinking horizon and from below at its exact orders on every horizon; it is needed only for the concentration of the driving covariances, the Gaussianity of the pair, and uniqueness, and what remains open is one-sided and above the leading order.

Heavy-tailed private information in global games
2025SSRNeconomic theoryheavy tails
Sangsidhya Kar
Abstract

The standard global-games argument has three components: sufficiently extreme private signals create dominance regions, best responses to cutoff strategies are themselves cutoffs, and diffuse public information selects the Laplacian cutoff. We study how these components depend on the distribution of private noise. Tweedie's formula links dominance to the posterior mean: dominance regions exist for every public precision exactly when the posterior mean can leave the unit interval. Cutoff best responses for all parameters are equivalent to log-concavity of the private-noise density.

For heavy-tailed noise with a polynomially bounded shift ratio, including generalized Pareto and Student-t families, both properties can fail. Extreme private signals are increasingly attributed to noise, and the posterior reverts to the public prior. When the public signal is sufficiently informative, both constant profiles are equilibria and every strategy is rationalizable. Under the same tail condition, cutoff equilibria are confined to the symmetric public signal, while a non-constant equilibrium with aggregate investment close to the complete-information mixed level exists for sufficiently informative public signals.

The diffuse-information limit is different: for symmetric noise, rationalizable strategies converge on compact sets to the Laplacian cutoff without a moment condition. Without symmetry, a finite mean is needed for this conclusion. For log-concave noise, diffuse public information also gives uniqueness once the public signal is sufficiently diffuse. Thus the usual selection argument separates into distinct properties of private information. A Gaussian approximation of a heavy-tailed posterior can therefore predict a unique cutoff equilibrium in parameter regions where the underlying game has complete-information-type multiplicity.
